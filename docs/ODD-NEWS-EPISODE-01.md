### Country 1: Uganda (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Uganda. East Africa. Population 48.7 million. GDP per capita: $1,002. Life expectancy: 68 years.

The median age in Uganda is 16. Half the country hasn't reached adulthood yet. That single number tells you everything about the future of this place — and about the pressure on the present.

In Kampala, the capital, a teacher earns about $150 to $200 a month. Rent for a one-bedroom apartment in the city center runs $100 to $150. That leaves $50 to $100 for food, transport, school fees, and everything else.

But here's what the GDP number doesn't tell you. Uganda is one of the youngest countries on Earth. The population has grown from 24 million in 2000 to nearly 49 million today. That's a country where half the people are children or teenagers.

And the poverty rate — the percentage of people living on less than $2.15 a day — has dropped from 38% in 2012 to about 21% today. That's millions of people who were poor a decade ago and aren't anymore.

In the markets of Kampala, matooke — green cooking bananas — cost about $1 per bunch. A kilogram of rice is 60 to 80 cents. A liter of milk is 40 cents. The cost of living is different from what you'd imagine from the GDP number alone.

Global Voices has reported on Ugandan farmers using mobile payment systems to sell crops directly to buyers, cutting out middlemen who used to take 40% of the profit. In the village of Mbale, a farmer named Joseph told the paper: "We used to wait for the broker. Now the money comes to my phone."

The life expectancy is 68 years. Twenty years ago, it was 47. That's one of the fastest improvements in human history. HIV treatment programs, malaria prevention, and better maternal care have added two decades to the average Ugandan life.

Forty-eight million people. Half of them under 16. Getting poorer faster than almost anywhere on Earth — and getting richer at the same time. Both things are true.

### Country 2: Nepal (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Nepal. South Asia. Population about 30 million. GDP per capita: roughly $1,300. Life expectancy: about 70 years.

Nepal sits between India and China, in the Himalayas. Mount Everest is here — the highest point on Earth. But most Nepalis don't live near Everest. They live in the valleys and plains, in cities like Kathmandu, Pokhara, and Biratnagar.

In Kathmandu, a schoolteacher earns about $150 to $250 a month. Rent for a small apartment in the city runs $100 to $200. Food is cheap — a meal at a local dal bhat restaurant costs $2 to $3. A liter of milk is about 50 cents.

Nepal's economy is built on three things: agriculture, remittances, and tourism. About 60% of the population works in agriculture — rice, wheat, corn, vegetables. Remittances from Nepalis working abroad — in Malaysia, Qatar, Saudi Arabia — account for about 25% of GDP. That's one of the highest remittance percentages in the world.

Tourism was devastated by the 2015 earthquake and has been slowly recovering. Trekking, mountaineering, and cultural tourism bring in about $500 million a year — significant for a country with a $40 billion economy.

The poverty rate has dropped from 43% in 2010 to about 20% today. Life expectancy has risen from 62 to 70 in the same period. Nepal is getting richer, slowly, and living longer, steadily.

But there's a tension. The young people are leaving. Nepal has one of the highest rates of youth emigration in the world. Young men go to the Gulf states for construction work. Young women go to Malaysia for factory work. Families are separated. Villages are aging.

The median age is 24. Half the country is under 24. But the people who are leaving are exactly the ones the country needs.

Global Voices reported recently that Nepali women are starting small businesses with money sent home by relatives abroad — tailoring shops, tea houses, small farms. Not big businesses. Just... enough.

A country with Everest on one side and the Ganges plain on the other. Getting richer, living longer, and watching its young people leave.

Sleep well.

### Country 3: Bolivia (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Bolivia. South America. Population 12.2 million. GDP per capita: $4,275. Life expectancy: 69 years.

Bolivia is landlocked. No ocean. It's bordered by Brazil, Peru, Chile, and Argentina. The capital is Sucre — though the government sits in La Paz, which is the highest capital city in the world at 3,640 meters above sea level.

La Paz is extraordinary. The city sits in a canyon. Cable cars — the Mi Teleférico system — crisscross above the streets like a sky metro. There are over 30 cable car lines, the largest urban cable car network on Earth. It costs about 30 cents to ride.

In La Paz, a schoolteacher earns about $300 to $400 a month. Rent for a small apartment runs $200 to $350. A meal at a local restaurant — salteñas, the famous Bolivian pastries — costs $1 to $2.

Bolivia's economy is built on natural gas, minerals, and agriculture. The country has the second-largest natural gas reserves in South America, after Venezuela. Lithium — the battery mineral — is found in the Salar de Uyuni, the largest salt flat on Earth.

The Salar de Uyuni is 10,582 square kilometers of salt crust. After rain, it becomes a giant mirror. Satellite images show it as a white void from space. It's one of the most photographed places on Earth.

Bolivia's poverty rate has dropped from 36% in 2010 to about 26% today. Life expectancy has risen from 65 to 69. The country has made real progress — but it's still one of the poorest in South America.

Evo Morales, the country's first Indigenous president, served from 2006 to 2019. His government expanded access to education, healthcare, and gas revenue sharing with poor regions. The country's poverty rate fell by a third during his tenure.

Global Voices has reported on Bolivian women's cooperatives in La Paz — groups of women who pool resources to buy raw materials, make textiles, and sell directly to tourists. Not a big industry. Just enough.

Twelve million people. The highest capital on Earth. Salt flat mirrors. Getting richer, one cooperative at a time.

Eighteen million people. The westernmost point of Africa. Stable democracy. Young and getting older. Growing slowly, and the young are watching.

Seventeen million people. Angkor Wat. A quarter of the population killed in a genocide 50 years ago. And now, one of the fastest poverty reductions on Earth. Both things are true.

### Country 6: Rwanda (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Rwanda. East Africa. Population 14 million. GDP per capita: $1,061. Life expectancy: 68 years.

Rwanda is small — the size of Maryland. It's called the Land of a Thousand Hills because the terrain is mountainous and green. The capital is Kigali, which is one of the cleanest cities in Africa.

Rwanda's story is one of the most remarkable in modern history. In 1994, a genocide killed about 800,000 people in 100 days. Tutsi and moderate Hutu. The international community watched and did nothing.

Three decades later, Rwanda has the highest GDP growth rate in Africa — averaging 7-8% per year. The poverty rate has dropped from 57% in 2001 to about 39% today. Life expectancy has risen from 28 in 1994 to 68 today. That's the fastest improvement in human history.

The government has invested heavily in technology, healthcare, and education. Kigali is becoming a tech hub — Rwanda has one of the fastest internet speeds in Africa. The government has banned plastic bags. Kigali is one of the cleanest cities on the continent.

But Rwanda is also an authoritarian state. President Paul Kagame has been in power since 2000. He's been praised for the economic transformation and criticized for suppressing political opposition. Both things are true.

Global Voices has reported on Rwandan women's cooperatives — groups that produce coffee, crafts, and agricultural products. Women make up about 60% of the workforce. The government has made gender equality a priority — Rwanda has more women in parliament than almost any other country.

Fourteen million people. A genocide 30 years ago. The fastest poverty reduction on Earth. An authoritarian state that's also a development success story. Both things are true.

### Country 8: Mongolia (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Mongolia. Central Asia. Population 3.5 million. GDP per capita: $5,839. Life expectancy: 72 years.

Mongolia is one of the least densely populated countries on Earth. Three and a half million people in 1.56 million square kilometers — that's about 2 people per square kilometer. For comparison, the United States has 33 people per square kilometer.

The capital is Ulaanbaatar, which holds about half the country's population. It's the coldest capital city on Earth — winter temperatures drop to -40°C. And it has a problem: air pollution. In winter, coal-burning stoves in the ger districts — the traditional felt tent neighborhoods — create smog so thick it's visible from space.

In Ulaanbaatar, a schoolteacher earns about $400 to $600 a month. Rent for a small apartment runs $300 to $500. A meal at a local restaurant — buuz, steamed dumplings — costs $2 to $4.

Mongolia's economy is built on mining — coal, copper, gold. The country sits on some of the largest mineral deposits in the world. The Oyu Tolgoi mine alone contains enough copper and gold to supply the world for decades.

But mining creates a boom-and-bust cycle. When commodity prices are high, Mongolia grows fast. When they drop, the economy contracts. The GDP per capita of $5,839 masks significant volatility.

The nomadic tradition continues. About 25% of Mongolians still live as herders — moving with their livestock across the steppe. They live in gers — the traditional felt tents. It's one of the last nomadic cultures on Earth.

Global Voices has reported on Mongolian women herders who are combining traditional practices with modern technology — using satellite phones to track weather, mobile banking to sell livestock, and social media to connect with urban markets.

Three and a half million people. Half of them in the coldest capital on Earth. The last nomadic culture. And a mining economy that makes them richer and poorer at the same time.

Ghana. West Africa. Population 33.8 million. GDP per capita: $2,384. Life expectancy: 65 years.

Ghana was the first sub-Saharan African country to gain independence, in 1957. Kwame Nkrumah, its first president, was a pan-Africanist who believed the continent's future lay in unity. That vision didn't fully materialize — but Ghana's stability did.

But like many resource-rich countries, Ghana faces the "resource curse" — wealth from natural resources doesn't always reach ordinary people. The poverty rate has dropped from 36% in 2012 to about 24% today, but inequality remains high.

Ghana has something many African countries don't: a functioning democracy. Peaceful transfers of power since 1992. An independent judiciary. A free press. It's not perfect — corruption exists, but the institutions are real.

Global Voices has reported on Ghanaian women's shea butter cooperatives — groups that produce shea butter from the nuts of the shea tree. The products are exported to Europe and North America for use in cosmetics. It's a $100 million industry that supports hundreds of thousands of women.

Thirty-three million people. The first African country to gain independence. Gold, cocoa, oil. A democracy that works. And poverty that's falling, slowly.

Seven and a half million people. The battery of Southeast Asia. Landlocked and developing. Young and urbanizing. Getting richer, one dam at a time.

### Country 16: Bhutan (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Bhutan. South Asia. Population 0.8 million. GDP per capita: $3,831. Life expectancy: 73 years.

Bhutan is one of the smallest countries on Earth — 800,000 people in 38,394 square kilometers. It's landlocked in the Himalayas, between China and India. The capital is Thimphu.

Bhutan is famous for Gross National Happiness — a development philosophy that prioritizes well-being over GDP. The government measures progress through GNH indicators: psychological well-being, health, education, time use, cultural diversity, good governance, community vitality, ecological diversity, and living standards.

In Thimphu, a schoolteacher earns about $200 to $400 a month. Rent for a small apartment runs $100 to $300. A plate of ema datshi — chili cheese, the national dish — costs $2 to $4.

Bhutan's economy is built on hydropower, agriculture, and tourism. The country exports electricity to India, which accounts for about 25% of GDP. Tourism is limited — the government deliberately keeps visitor numbers low to protect the environment and culture.

The poverty rate has dropped from 23% in 2010 to about 12% today. Life expectancy has risen from 67 to 73. Bhutan is developing, but it's doing it on its own terms — rejecting consumerism, protecting its environment, and prioritizing happiness over growth.

Bhutan is also the only carbon-negative country on Earth. The forests absorb more carbon dioxide than the country produces. The constitution mandates that at least 60% of the country remain forested forever.

Global Voices has reported on Bhutanese women's weaving cooperatives — groups that produce traditional textiles using patterns that date back centuries. The textiles are central to Bhutanese culture — the national dress, the kira, is handwoven by women.

Eight hundred thousand people. The happiest country on Earth. Carbon-negative. And a development model that the rest of the world is watching.

Sleep well.

### Country 17: Suriname (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Suriname. South America. Population 0.6 million. GDP per capita: $5,522. Life expectancy: 74 years.

Suriname is one of the smallest countries in South America — 600,000 people in 163,820 square kilometers. It's on the northeastern coast, bordered by Guyana, French Guiana, and Brazil. The capital is Paramaribo.

Suriname is unique: it's a former Dutch colony, and Dutch is still the official language. But the population is one of the most diverse on Earth — Hindustani, Creole, Javanese, Chinese, Indigenous, and Maroon communities coexist.

In Paramaribo, a schoolteacher earns about $300 to $500 a month. Rent for a small apartment runs $200 to $400. A meal at a local restaurant — roti with chicken — costs $2 to $4.

Suriname's economy is built on mining — gold and bauxite. The country is one of the world's largest producers of bauxite, the raw material for aluminum. Gold mining has been growing, but it comes with environmental costs — mercury pollution in rivers, deforestation in the interior.

The poverty rate is about 30% — relatively high for a country with a GDP per capita of $5,522. The wealth from mining doesn't always reach ordinary people, especially in the interior where Maroon and Indigenous communities live.

Suriname has something extraordinary: the Central Suriname Nature Reserve — a UNESCO World Heritage Site covering 1.6 million hectares of pristine tropical rainforest. It's one of the largest protected areas in the world.

Global Voices has reported on Surinamese Maroon communities — descendants of escaped slaves who built independent societies in the rainforest. They have their own languages, cultures, and governance systems. Their knowledge of the forest is irreplaceable.

Six hundred thousand people. Dutch in South America. The most diverse country on Earth. And rainforest that's still intact.

Sleep well.

### Country 18: Bolivia (Deep Dive) (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Let's return to Bolivia — but this time, we'll look deeper.

Bolivia is named after Simón Bolívar — the "Liberator" who led independence movements across South America. But Bolivia is not a romantic country. It's poor, landlocked, and high-altitude. The poverty rate is 26%. Life expectancy is 69.

And yet — Bolivia has done something remarkable. In 2006, Evo Morales became the country's first Indigenous president. He was a coca farmer, a union leader, a man who spoke Aymara — not Spanish — before he learned Spanish at age 12.

Morales nationalized the natural gas industry, increasing government revenue by billions. He expanded access to education, healthcare, and gas revenue sharing with poor regions. The poverty rate fell by a third during his tenure.

But Morales was also controversial. He changed the constitution to allow unlimited re-elections. He suppressed media. He was accused of corruption. In 2019, he was forced to resign after disputed elections.

Since then, Bolivia has had political turmoil — but also stability. The MAS party, Morales's movement, returned to power in 2020. The economy continues to grow. The poverty rate continues to fall.

Bolivia's lithium reserves — in the Salar de Uyuni — could power the world's electric vehicles for decades. The government is negotiating with foreign companies to develop them. Whether the wealth will reach ordinary Bolivians is the open question.

Global Voices has reported on Bolivian women's water rights movements — groups fighting for access to clean water in a country where water is a scarce resource. The "Water Wars" of 2000 were a turning point in Bolivian politics.

Twelve million people. Indigenous president. Lithium reserves. And a question that every resource-rich country faces: who gets the wealth?

Sleep well.

### Country 19: Nepal (Deep Dive) (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Let's return to Nepal.

Nepal is home to Mount Everest — the highest point on Earth, at 8,849 meters. But Everest is a distraction. The real Nepal is in the Terai — the flat southern plains where 60% of the population lives.

In the Terai, farmers grow rice, wheat, and mustard. They live in mud-brick houses. They send their children to school — but the schools are underfunded. The teacher-to-student ratio is one of the highest in the world.

In Kathmandu, the story is different. The city is growing fast — construction cranes everywhere, new apartments rising, traffic jams worsening. The GDP per capita is $1,300, but Kathmandu feels richer than that.

Nepal's biggest export is not goods — it's people. Remittances account for 25% of GDP. Nepalis work in Malaysia, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and South Korea. They send money home. Families survive on it.

But remittances come at a cost. Children grow up without parents. Villages empty out. The median age is 24 — but the people who leave are exactly the ones the country needs.

Global Voices has reported on Nepali women's textile cooperatives — groups that produce traditional dhaka fabric, woven on handlooms. The products are sold to tourists and exported. It's preserving a craft and providing income in rural areas.

Nepal also has something the world rarely sees: a living goddess — the Kumari. A young girl, chosen for her purity, is worshipped as the living embodiment of the goddess Taleju. She lives in a temple in Kathmandu until her first menstruation, when she returns to ordinary life.

Thirty million people. Everest. The Kumari. And remittances that keep the economy running.

Sleep well.

### Country 20: Tanzania (Deep Dive) (900 words, ~6 minutes)

Let's return to Tanzania.

Tanzania is home to the Serengeti — where millions of wildebeest and zebras migrate across the plains each year. It's one of the most spectacular wildlife events on Earth. And it's under threat.

Climate change is altering rainfall patterns. Droughts are becoming more frequent. The migration is shifting — some herds are moving earlier, some are moving less. The ecosystem is adapting, but slowly.

In Dar es Salaam, the story is different. The city is growing fast — construction everywhere, new apartments rising, traffic jams worsening. The GDP per capita is $1,224, but Dar es Salaam feels richer than that.

Tanzania's economy is built on agriculture — cashews, coffee, cotton, and tobacco. Mining — gold, tanzanite, diamonds — is growing. Tourism — safaris, Kilimanjaro, Zanzibar — brings in $2.5 billion a year.

But like many African countries, Tanzania faces the resource curse. The wealth from mining and tourism doesn't always reach ordinary people. The poverty rate is 26%. Life expectancy is 67. Progress is real, but slow.

Tanzania has something extraordinary: Zanzibar. The island off the coast is famous for its spices, its beaches, and its history as a trading hub. Stone Town — the old city — is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, with Arabic, Indian, and European architecture.

Global Voices has reported on Tanzanian women's cashew cooperatives — groups that produce cashews, which are exported worldwide. The cooperatives pool resources to buy processing equipment, cutting out middlemen and keeping more profit in the community.

Sixty-six million people. Kilimanjaro. The Serengeti. Zanzibar. And a population that's growing faster than the economy.

Sleep well.
