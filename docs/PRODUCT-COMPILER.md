# OddHobb — The Product Compiler

> One researched procedure → free tutorial → digital files → physical tool → kit → premium version.
> Saved verbatim from research.

---

The important realization is that this is not merely a content funnel.

It is a **product compiler**:

**one researched procedure → free tutorial → digital manufacturing files → physical tool/object → kit → premium finished version.**

That works best when the hobby contains **geometry**: patterns, dimensions, jigs, templates, fixtures, holders, repeatable assemblies or standardized components. Current marketplace data supports exactly those categories. Cults, for example, supports STL/STEP/SCAD for printing plus DXF/SVG/PDF for CNC, laser cutting, sewing and papercraft, while its current best-seller pages include board-game inserts, machine adapters and functional fixtures—not just figurines.

And the 2026 trend backdrop is excellent: Pinterest reports Gen Z shifting toward tactile/offline hobbies, with cyberdecks +1,410%, hand embroidery +1,351%, goth pottery +479%, model-car kits +426% and mini-room crafts +296%.

The current market evidence on several of these is unusually concrete.

**Cyberdecks are almost made for this.** Pinterest currently has cyberdeck searches up 1,410%, and Etsy already has a market of digital cyberdeck cases and kits: a Pi Zero laptop STL + BOM + instructions sells around $10, while complete printed frames and systems go considerably higher. The perfect bundle is therefore:

**video → STL + STEP + BOM + wiring schematic + assembly PDF → printed shell → complete hardware kit.**

That could easily become **OddHobb / Cyberdeck** rather than one product.

**Bookbinding is perhaps the cleanest low-risk version.** Etsy currently shows 130+ bookbinding-jig products, with examples including a miniature binder tool with 5.1k reviews, physical jig sets around $13–20 and digital STL jig sets with hundreds to more than a thousand reviews. One tutorial can naturally generate every artefact:

> How to bind a miniature hardcover book
> → cutting template
> → spine calculator
> → corner jig STL
> → punching cradle STL
> → pictogram manual
> → physical jig kit
> → finished blank book.

That is nearly perfect.

**Stained glass is the best SVG-first manifestation.** Etsy currently has 4,000+ stained-glass SVG pattern listings; individual original patterns commonly bundle PDF + SVG and sell around the $5–8 region, while active pattern shops have hundreds of reviews.

But we can make the pack better than merely "a pattern":

**SVG master**
+ numbered printable cutting pattern
+ foil-width guide
+ colour plan
+ solder sequence
+ 3D-printed 90°/angle positioning jigs
+ pictogram assembly manual
+ tutorial video.

Then sell either **the digital maker pack** or **the finished glass object**.

That is exactly the maker/buyer split you identified.

**Pottery is massive but needs better differentiation.** Etsy currently shows 5,000+ pottery texture-roller listings; physical rollers have thousands of reviews, and digital roller files sell from a few dollars through roughly $14, including current 2026 listings. So I would not sell "flower roller #847."

I'd build a **system**:

> tutorial: make a Gothic apothecary mug
> digital: 4 roller sleeves + handle STL + cutting template + handle template
> physical: reusable roller handle + 4 sleeves
> premium: full pottery template kit.

The **interchangeable chassis** differentiates it from giant cheap STL bundles.

**Needlepoint is particularly interesting because the physical kit economics are already high.** Etsy currently has 5,000+ beginner-kit results, with many complete kits around $30–75 and individual successful listings with thousands of reviews. Digital stitch references also sell.

So make each tutorial a tiny finished project:

> "Stitch a tiny medieval frog"

Free video.

Then:

**$5–9** chart + stitch guide
**$12–18** chart + printable organizer + finishing pattern
**$25–40** physical canvas/tool pack
**$40–60** complete kit.

And create original art rather than the huge amount of branded/copyright-dependent needlepoint currently floating around Etsy.

**Mahjong might be the most commercially obvious hobby-product vertical.** Etsy currently has 133+ results specifically for 3D-printed Mahjong racks, including $8.99 digital magnetic rack files, $25–30 physical travel racks and a $49 printable tile system with hundreds of reviews.

A tutorial like:

> **Build the Perfect Travel Mahjong Setup**

can emit:

**rack STL**
**pusher STL**
**dealer/wind marker**
**travel box**
**assembly PDF**
**strategy/reference printable**

and the physical £30–50 version.

### One really powerful distinction: sell the **project**, not the file

Instead of:

> STL — Cyberdeck Case — $7

OddHobb sells:

> **Build Your Own Pocket Cyberdeck — Project Pack**

containing:

**STEP** — editable master
**STL/3MF** — ready to print
**SVG/DXF** — panels/cut pieces
**PDF** — visual manual
**BOM** — exact hardware
**source file** — OpenSCAD where appropriate
**video** — full walkthrough
**version history** — updated file compatibility.

Then the customer thinks they're buying a **complete project**, not a commodity file.

That lets you plausibly charge **$15–30** where random STL marketplaces are fighting at $2–5.

And there is evidence people pay more for technically useful files. Current Etsy woodworking-jig listings include digital router templates at ~$6–20 with hundreds or thousands of reviews; bookbinding STL sets similarly sell in multi-size bundles.

### The part I'd change in your stack

Don't let AI-generated CAD become the authoritative master without testing.

AI can get us from **idea → candidate geometry ridiculously fast**, but functional products need:

**generate → slice → print prototype → measure → revise → freeze v1 → publish.**

Especially:

- jigs used near blades/routers;
- electrical enclosures;
- load-bearing mounts;
- watchmaking precision fixtures;
- anything heated;
- anything children might use.

For decorative stands, patterns, trays and low-load fixtures, iteration can be much lighter.

I'd also develop an **OddHobb visual-manual language** rather than literally mimicking IKEA. Same strengths—monochrome isometric panels, hands/arrows, low text—but recognizably ours.

### The really insane extension

A project doesn't need three revenue streams.

It can have **six**:

**YouTube tutorial** — free discovery
→ **digital project pack** — $8–25
→ **physical tool/object** — £15–50
→ **complete starter kit** — £30–100
→ **sleepy companion video** — audience/discovery
→ **OddHobb Records CD** — physical collectible.

Take watchmaking:

> **How to Service an NH35**

produces an exploded technical video, movement-holder STL, labeled screw tray, printable parts map, visual manual, physical tool set, a "Quiet Watchmaker's Bench" sleepy episode and potentially the CD we just discussed.

Or bookbinding:

> **Make a Medieval Pocket Book**

produces a 30-minute tutorial, patterns, jigs, project PDF, physical kit, a two-hour sleepy history of bookbinding, and eventually a physical reproduction/book.

That means **tutorial research is no longer a marketing cost. It is the raw material from which the entire product tree is generated.**

The first five I'd actually build are **Bookbinding project #1 → Stained Glass project #1 → Cyberdeck project #1 → Mahjong project #1 → Needlepoint/Sashiko project #1**. Those collectively test STL, SVG, PDF, electronics, physical kits, patterns and outsourced fulfilment without forcing us into safety-critical products.
