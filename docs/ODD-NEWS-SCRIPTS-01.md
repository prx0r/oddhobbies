**[BODY — 0:25-8:00]**

**Story 1: Sea Turtles on the West Coast (0:25-1:45)**

For the first time ever, olive ridley sea turtles have nested on California's coast. Not somewhere in Mexico or Costa Rica. California. The LA Times reported that the female turtles laid their eggs on Southern California beaches — the first recorded nesting on America's entire West Coast.

Scientists aren't sure why they came this far north. Maybe the ocean currents shifted. Maybe the water got warmer. But they came. And the eggs are there. And somewhere, tiny sea turtles are going to hatch on a beach they were never supposed to reach.

**Story 2: The 12-Year-Old's Diaper Drive (1:45-3:00)**

A food bank in Phoenix, Arizona got a surprise visit. A U-Haul truck pulled up, packed to the ceiling with diapers. The driver was 12 years old. Her name is Sloan, and this was her fifth annual diaper drive.

She started when she was seven. Five years later, she's collected and delivered 30,000 diapers. Not for a school project. Not for social media. Because she noticed that families at the food bank needed diapers and decided to do something about it.

**Story 3: Vietnam Protects the World's Rarest Monkey (3:00-4:15)**

In Vietnam, the government has created a new conservation reserve — 7,500 acres of limestone forest — to protect the Delacour's langur. There are fewer than 200 of these monkeys left on Earth. They live in the karst mountains of northern Vietnam, and their habitat has been shrinking for decades.

The new reserve is named after a French biologist who first described the species in 1932. His name lives on in the forest that will keep his monkeys alive.

**Story 4: Norway's New Ocean Sanctuary (4:15-5:30)**

Norway just protected 714 square miles of ocean. That's 260,000 football pitches. The Andfjorden Marine Protected Area shelters whales, seabirds, and one of the richest marine habitats in the North Atlantic.

Norway is an oil country. They drill for fossil fuels in the North Sea. And they just protected a chunk of the ocean next door. Progress isn't a straight line. But it's happening.

**Story 5: The Archaeologists Who Saved History (5:30-7:00)**

In 2014, as ISIS closed in on the city of Deir ez-Zor in Syria, archaeologists made a choice. They risked their lives to evacuate 30,000 artifacts from the museum. Ancient tablets, statues, ceramics — thousands of years of human history, packed into trucks and smuggled out of a war zone.

A new study details how they did it. They worked through the night. They hid the most precious pieces in private homes. They lied to armed checkpoints. And when the city fell, the museum was destroyed — but the artifacts survived.

## Script 3: "Goodnight News — The World's Smallest Wins"

**Duration:** 8-10 minutes
**Sources:** Good News Network + Unignorant
**Product:** Personalized gifts + cards
**Voice:** Warm, intimate, conversational

---

[LOGO INTRO — 2 seconds]

**[HOOK — 0:00-0:25]**

Tonight, I want to tell you about the smallest wins I could find. Not the billion-dollar donations. Not the world records. The tiny, quiet, barely-noticed things that happened this week. And why they matter more than you think.

**[BODY — 0:25-8:00]**

**Story 1: The Frog Named After Mom (0:25-1:45)**

Two student scientists in Chicago discovered a new species. A frog-like creature that lived 300 million years before the dinosaurs. And they named it after their moms.

Allison and Payton found the fossil in Illinois. They described the species — the scientific process of naming something new. And when they got to the naming part, they thought: who should this be for?

Not a famous scientist. Not a museum. Their moms.

The frog is now in the scientific record. Somewhere, two mothers are the namesakes of a creature that hasn't existed for 300 million years. That's not a headline. But it's everything.

**Story 2: The Painting Worth $250,000 (1:45-3:00)**

At an estate sale in Michigan, someone noticed a painting hanging in a hallway. It showed seashells on a dark background. Nothing special. But the signature read "Abercrombie '51."

The buyer didn't know who Abercrombie was. But they spent $30 and bought it anyway. When they got home, they looked it up. Gertrude Abercrombie was a significant American painter. The painting could be worth $250,000.

Thirty dollars. A hallway at an estate sale. A signature that caught someone's eye. Sometimes luck is just paying attention.

**Story 3: The Bees That Learned to Avoid Pesticides (3:00-4:30)**

Researchers at UC Riverside found something elegant. Certain chemical compounds can safely repel honey bees from pesticide-treated crops. Not kill them. Just... politely ask them to go somewhere else.

It's a small discovery. But bees are dying. And every small tool helps. A solution doesn't have to be dramatic to be important. It just has to work.

**Story 4: Cork That Insulates Spacecraft (4:30-5:45)**

A 150-year-old Portuguese company that makes corks just showed the world that old businesses can innovate. Corticeira Amorim has transformed cork — the humble material that seals wine bottles — into a technology that can insulate spacecraft.

Same trees. Same forests. Same company that's been harvesting cork since the monarchy. Just... different application. Progress isn't always new. Sometimes it's old things used in new ways.

**Story 5: Bikes Beat Cars in Edinburgh (5:45-7:00)**

For the first time in history, bicycles outnumbered cars during rush hour in Edinburgh. Not in Amsterdam. Not in Copenhagen. Edinburgh. The Scottish capital.

The data came from Cycling Scotland. E-bike rentals and segregated bike lanes have made cycling so popular that the bikes won. Literally outnumbered the cars.

Scotland. Rain. Cold. And they chose to ride bikes anyway.
