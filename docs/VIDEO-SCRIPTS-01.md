**Section 1: The Problem with Dreams (0:30-2:00)**

Modern sleep science actually backs this up. When you set an intention before sleep, your reticular activating system — the part of your brain that filters what matters — starts looking for relevant information during REM sleep.

The object becomes an anchor. The ritual becomes a signal. Your brain knows: this sleep is different. Pay attention.

Studies at MIT and Harvard have shown that people who keep dream journals near their bed remember up to three times more dreams than people who don't. The act of writing trains your brain to value dream memory.

You don't need a temple. You need:

1. A clear intention before sleep

## Script 2: Odd Minds — "Why Your ADHD Brain Isn't Broken"

**Duration:** 8-10 minutes
**Product:** Energy Planner ($12.99) + Task Cards ($9.99)
**Voice:** Curious, warm, slightly playful

---

[LOGO INTRO — 2 seconds]

**[HOOK — 0:00-0:30]**

Your ADHD brain isn't broken. It's just running an operating system that the rest of the world wasn't designed for.

I know that sounds like something a motivational poster would say. But there's actual neuroscience behind it. And once you understand what your brain is actually doing, everything changes.

**[BODY — 0:30-7:00]**

**Section 1: The Dopamine Problem (0:30-2:00)**

Here's what's happening in your head. Your brain produces dopamine — the chemical that makes you feel motivated, focused, and rewarded. In a neurotypical brain, dopamine flows steadily. Task begins → dopamine releases → you feel motivated → task completes → you feel rewarded.

In an ADHD brain, that dopamine signal is inconsistent. Sometimes it's strong, sometimes it's absent. This isn't a character flaw. It's a neurological difference. Your brain's reward system just doesn't work on the same schedule as everyone else's.

This is why you can hyperfocus on something you love for six hours and can't start a simple email. It's not laziness. It's chemistry.

**Section 2: The Interest-Based Nervous System (2:00-4:00)**

Most productivity advice is built for importance-based brains. "Do this because it's important." "Do this because it's due tomorrow."

ADHD brains run on interest-based nervous systems. You do things because they're interesting, challenging, urgent, or novel. Not because they're important.

This isn't a bug. Some of the most creative, innovative people in history had ADHD brains — Einstein, Da Vinci, Mozart, Richard Branson. They didn't succeed despite their ADHD. In some ways, they succeeded because of it.

The problem isn't that your brain works differently. The problem is that everyone expects you to work the same way.

**Section 3: The Time Blindness Factor (4:00-5:30)**

ADHD brains experience time differently. Psychologists call it "time blindness" — the inability to feel how much time has passed.

This is why:
- 5 minutes feels like 2 hours (boring task)
- 2 hours feels like 5 minutes (interesting task)
- Deadlines feel abstract until they're urgent
- "I'll do it later" becomes "I'll never do it"

Time blindness isn't a discipline problem. It's a perception problem. Your brain literally doesn't feel time the same way.

**Section 4: The Solution Isn't Willpower (5:30-7:00)**

Here's what actually works. Not trying harder. Not waking up earlier. Not reading another productivity book.

1. **Externalize time.** Use visual timers, alarms, physical clocks. Make time visible because your brain won't feel it.

2. **Reduce friction.** Make good habits easy and bad habits hard. Lay out your clothes. Set up your workspace. Remove barriers between you and starting.

3. **Work with your energy, not against it.** Do high-energy tasks when you have energy. Do low-energy tasks when you don't. Stop trying to force everything into the same schedule.

4. **Make it interesting.** Add music. Change locations. Use timers as challenges. Gamify boring tasks.

5. **Be kind to yourself.** Shame doesn't motivate ADHD brains. It makes everything worse. Self-compassion actually improves focus.
